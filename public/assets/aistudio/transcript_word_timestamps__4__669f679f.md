Story summary: A con man runs a fake lottery, and every person who replies to his email costs him an hour on the phone. He writes his scam email with 3 spelling mistakes in the first line on purpose. His partner Craig is sure nobody will fall for it, so he rewrites his own version in perfect English with a shiny logo. The next morning, each man emails 8,700 strangers. By noon, Craig has 640 replies and dances around the office in his socks, while the con man has 6 and just refills his coffee. Craig says "640 to 6" and reaches for the phone. The con man opens his first reply. While Craig dials his first call, the con man reads a "Yes" from a man who spotted the misspellings and replied anyway. The concept is false positives: a polished message attracts skeptics who never pay.

What's really going on: The three spelling mistakes look like carelessness, but they are a filter. The con man's real cost is not sending emails, which costs him almost nothing. His real cost is the hour he spends on the phone with every person who replies. So his goal is not the most replies. His goal is replies only from people who will actually pay. A person who notices 3 mistakes in the first line and still replies is showing that they are not checking carefully. A person who checks carefully deletes the email, and that saves him an hour. If the email were perfect, careful skeptics would reply, because nothing would put them off. In the story's terms, those skeptics are the false positives: people who respond but will never pay.

Every number and object has a job. The 8,700 strangers is the same size list for both men, so the comparison is fair. The 640 and the 6 count replies, but replies are not the score that matters. Each reply costs an hour, so 640 replies means 640 hours of calls, and almost all of them are with people who will never pay. The 6 replies are cheap, and mostly come from people who are ready. The perfect English and the shiny logo are Craig's attempt to look trustworthy, and that is exactly what draws in the doubters. The phone is the key object at the end: Craig reaches for it to start his hours, while the con man only has to read one message.

Before the reveal, Craig appears to be winning. He dances in his socks, and the score is 640 to 6. In reality he is losing, because every one of his replies costs time and brings little. The con man looks calm, refilling his coffee, because he knows replies are the wrong thing to count. The final "Yes" comes from a man who squinted at 3 misspelled words, shrugged and replied anyway. He is the ready buyer, exactly who the mistakes were built to find, and his answer arrives while Craig is only dialing his first call. Craig stands for the common mistake of treating more responses as more success.

The concept works like a net. A wide, smooth net catches everyone, including people who never intended to buy. A net with an odd feature catches fewer, but the ones it catches looked past the oddity. The odd feature costs some leads, but it saves far more wasted time. Researchers say a smooth email pulls in skeptics who will never pay, but a silly one scares most of them off, so mostly the ready ones reply.

In real business, many owners get this backwards. They book as many sales calls as possible with a polished page that appeals to everyone, then spend the week on calls with people who never buy. A clear price and a clear "not for you" work like the spelling mistakes. A service business could show its minimum price on its booking page and say who it is not for. The calendar fills more slowly, but the people who book are ready to buy.
